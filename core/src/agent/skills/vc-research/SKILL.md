# VC research

This workflow supplies domain judgment, not an execution budget. Respect the selected light/deep mode, available tools and source scope.
Use the shared research skill for discovery mechanics and durable notes when
needed; do not duplicate its bookkeeping or add another completion gate.

- Identify the current job: opportunity discovery by default; meeting preparation
  or diligence only when requested. Do not automatically run every stage.
  Infer useful starting directions without inventing stage, geography, funding,
  check size or traction requirements. Follow-ups address the new request and
  affected findings, not a restart of completed work.
- Calibrate novelty for an experienced VC before recommending: assess whether
  the direction and projects are already widely discussed using observed dates,
  repeated coverage and ecosystem activity. Familiar or already somewhat hot
  projects are brief background/comparables, not the main recommendations.
  Seek less-discussed builders, emerging wedges and non-obvious implications.
  Low visibility alone is not merit; explain the substantive signal. Popularity
  is a provisional assessment, not a hard filter or an exhaustive survey.
- When sources are automatic and the request leaves platforms unspecified or
  the scope broad, default to Xiaohongshu, X, WeChat public articles, podcasts
  and GitHub. Explicit user platforms always take precedence. For discovery,
  delegate complementary platform questions together with explore_platforms;
  synthesize an opportunity map, then send focused follow-up questions with
  known findings and gaps in a later round. Do not restart all five on a narrow
  follow-up. Note unavailable sources; never claim coverage from failed workers.
- For sourcing, find people worth learning about: demos, repositories, early
  users, hiring, cofounder requests, unusual approaches and practitioner
  discussions. Explore complementary directions and follow unexpected relevant
  signals. Unknown identity, funding or revenue is a label, not an admission
  gate. Keep useful small and unnamed projects alongside the shortlist.
- Let the question and findings determine the report structure.
  Useful incomplete leads belong in the main findings with a concise uncertainty
  label and source, not hidden under coverage gaps. Reserve coverage gaps for
  genuinely unsearched or inaccessible areas. Lack of funding, founder identity,
  or complete product detail is not a reason to suppress a substantive signal.
- A name-only mention or ambiguous identity is an early signal when relevant:
  give the observed name, why it matters, the source and what is unknown in one
  compact entry. Do not infer that an unverified project is fake or nonexistent.
  Low interaction, no third-party coverage, missing founder names or an unclear
  startup stage alone do not justify exclusion. Label promotional/self-reported
  material and any concrete contradictory evidence separately. Avoid unsupported
  claims like "the most obscure project on the internet" or market-wide cooling
  inferred from a single post. Do not print internal coverage approval/gate text.
- Build an opportunity map around problems, approaches, builders and emerging
  signals. Include a compact candidate list with why each matters, an observed
  source link, uncertainties and a worthwhile next step. Scale detail to the
  request and budget; do not require a large report or graph for every answer.
- Connect people, projects and organizations using observed links. Distinguish
  author, founder, employee, curator and guest. Preserve uncertain identity
  matches as hypotheses; do not merge on name alone. Show a relationship table
  or graph only when useful, with sources for asserted relationships.
- With multiple allowed platforms, follow promising links across them when
  useful: a demo can lead to a repository, a builder profile or an interview.
  Do not verify every candidate on every platform. Reposts are not independent
  confirmation. A podcast description is not a listened-to transcript; a search
  preview is not a full read. Separate source claims, observed activity and
  inference, with relevant dates and contradictions.
- For tracking, suggest specific signals, sources and a sensible cadence. These
  are recommendations, not a running monitor. For meeting preparation, suggest
  focused questions tied to unknowns; draft outreach only when requested and
  never send or contact anyone without explicit authorization.
- For explicit diligence, investigate the user's decisive questions and keep
  missing evidence visible. Do not promote a promising lead to a verified,
  investable or fundraising company. Discovery does not require diligence first.

When update_research_board is available, record new leads as they arrive and
show what is being followed on each platform. Use signal/following for incomplete
findings; context for familiar comparables. In deep mode, publish a findings
table (CSV or XLSX) covering both priority and early leads, with source links,
uncertainties and next steps. Do not make table delivery a reason to prune leads.

## Research lenses

Choose lenses that fit the user's question; these are optional angles, not a
checklist or a fixed report structure. Let useful discoveries guide follow-ups.

- **Overlooked teams:** connect founders, products and community feedback across
  sources to find less-exposed teams with meaningful early traction. Explain
  why a meeting could be worthwhile; distinguish usage from attention and
  self-reported growth. Compare with the user's known projects when available.
- **Founder portrait:** trace public work, interviews, posts and collaborations
  over time. Look for technical preferences, changing views and contradictions,
  beyond a résumé. Cite dated examples; label interpretations of views or
  personality as inferences.
- **Ecosystem influence:** follow consequential work, citations, discussions and
  cross-project collaboration, not just follower counts. Surface less-visible
  contributors and evidenced connection paths; a public connection is not a
  confirmed willingness to introduce someone.
- **Emerging demand:** look for recurring problems across independent users and
  communities, when they appeared, and signs of increasing interest. Connect
  them to product opportunities; distinguish event dates from publication dates
  and observed changes from claims about the whole market.
- **Continuing discovery:** use available research history, seen teams and user
  preferences to highlight new opportunities or meaningful changes. Learn from
  explicit feedback without treating preferences as permanent exclusions. When
  ongoing discovery is requested, use supported scheduling/memory capabilities;
  otherwise explain what is available rather than imply monitoring is active.

## Priority actions

Close a substantive research report with 3–5 concise, high-priority actions,
ranked by expected learning or opportunity value. Tie each to a named lead or
specific observed signal and explain the question it would resolve in one short
sentence. Choose from: whom to contact or meet and why; a promising lead to dig
into; a decisive diligence question or product test; a relevant customer or
expert interview; or a concrete milestone to watch before revisiting a team.
Prefer the next useful decision over a generic checklist. Preserve uncertainty
about identities and contact routes; link known public profiles or sources rather
than inventing contact details. Suggest outreach only—do not contact anyone or
start monitoring without authorization. Do not expand this into a full diligence
plan unless requested, or force 3 actions into a simple factual follow-up.
